The context right now

Schools compete for workers with every other employer. An unfilled position means something different when one job in thirteen is open across the economy than when openings are scarce. So the first question about any "shortage" is how tight the whole labor market is.

Education and the whole labor market since 2000

The Job Openings and Labor Turnover Survey (JOLTS) has no category for public schools. The closest is state & local government education: public school districts plus public colleges and universities, all occupations pooled together. It is shown below against all sectors and against the rest of state and local government.

How education compares with other industries

Industry is not occupation. The education industry employs bus drivers, custodians, and administrators as well as teachers, and some teachers work outside it. All staff is not teachers. A change in the pooled education rate is mostly not a change in teacher vacancies specifically. More below.

How many people public education employs

Vacancies are only half the picture. A school system can report more openings because it is trying to employ more people. The chart indexes employment to February 2020, the last month before the pandemic.

What states actually count

No federal administrative collection counts unfilled teacher or school-staff positions. What exists is a patchwork of state surveys, and only a minority of states publish anything usable. This panel uses five states that publish rates (Illinois, Virginia, Iowa, South Carolina, Colorado) and one that publishes counts only (Nebraska). Each is an annual snapshot published months later, and each state defines "unfilled" its own way. Recent figures were checked against each agency's own publication, and the provenance page records exactly what was verified and what was carried over from earlier work. None is interpolated.

What counts as a "vacancy"

The word means different things in different sources. Some count positions empty on one day; one counts a year of unfilled job postings; one counts only positions empty all year. Some treat a position covered by a substitute as filled, others as vacant. Read every number below with its definition.

Latest rate and trend by state

Read trends within a state, not rankings across states. Colorado counts only positions unfilled for the whole year. Illinois and Virginia count an October stock. Iowa counts full-time teaching positions, including those filled by substitutes. South Carolina includes counselors, librarians, and other service staff. Nebraska combines public and private schools. These are related measures, not the same measure, and six states are not a national estimate. The table is in alphabetical order for that reason.

Teachers specifically

Pooled figures hide which jobs are hard to fill. Illinois publishes filled and unfilled positions by job category, and the teaching rate is not the highest.

"Not fully qualified" is not "vacant"

A position filled by a teacher on an emergency or provisional license, assigned out of field, or awaiting reciprocity is filled. "Not fully qualified" is an administrative credential category. It is not a vacancy, and the two should never be added together. On its own, the category does not establish a quality problem either.

What these data cannot say

  • There is no ongoing national teacher-vacancy count. JOLTS measures industries, not occupations. NCES's School Pulse Panel, a survey of public schools, estimated that about 3% of teaching positions were vacant in October 2023 and October 2024 (4% in October 2022), but its last staffing wave was October 2024. Any other national teacher figure is either a model (like the split above, labeled as one) or an aggregation of unlike state definitions.
  • State levels are not cross-state rankings. Definitions and frames differ. Trends within a state over time are the defensible comparison.
  • Annual snapshots cannot show within-year dynamics. State surveys observe one fall date per year, published three to six months later. They cannot show positions that were hard to fill but were filled by October.
  • A vacancy is not a quality judgment, and neither is a credential category. These data count empty budgeted positions and, separately, credential status. They do not measure how well students were taught.
  • National shortages can coexist with local ones. Averages can hide severe shortages in particular subjects (such as special education), places, or schools.
  • Computed rates carry their conventions. Where this page computes a rate from published components, it says so, and the components are in the downloadable data.
  • JOLTS is revised. The latest month is preliminary and each month is revised once. Annual benchmark revisions can change the whole history. This page re-pulls the full history every update.

Methods, sources, and data

Rates. Every rate on this page has the same form: openings (or unfilled positions) ÷ (employment + openings), or unfilled ÷ (filled + unfilled), as a percent. Because it is a rate rather than a count, it can be compared across time and across industries of very different sizes.

National data come from the BLS Job Openings and Labor Turnover Survey and Current Employment Statistics, retrieved from FRED (series JTSJOR, JTSJOL, JTSHIR, JTSQUR, JTSLDR, PAYEMS, UNRATE, UNEMPLOY, CES9093161101, CES9092161101) and the BLS Public Data API (industry series JTSiiiiii000000000XXX, including 923000 state & local education and 929000 state & local excluding education). Seasonally adjusted except the October state benchmarks, which use the not-seasonally-adjusted series JTU923000000000000JOR and JTU000000000000000JOR. An automated job refreshes these data weekly, so new JOLTS releases appear within days.

School Pulse Panel estimates come from the NCES "Staff Vacancies" workbook (nces.ed.gov/surveys/spp), re-checked automatically for new waves. They are shown as NCES publishes them: percent of positions vacant, rounded to whole percents, with standard errors used for the confidence intervals in tooltips.

State data are curated by hand from state agency publications (sources listed under each state above) and updated as states release new figures. The provenance page records, state by state, which figures were checked against the agency's own publication and which were carried over without re-checking. Where a state publishes a rate, the published rate is shown. Where it publishes only the components, the rate is computed and labeled. Where no denominator exists, no rate is shown.

Full documentation of every series (who publishes it, the exact file, how it is retrieved, every transformation, and known problems) is on the data provenance page.

Composition split. The rough split of state & local education openings is a model. It assigns openings pro-rata using the CES local/state government education employment shares (local is overwhelmingly K-12, state overwhelmingly higher education) and the NCES Common Core of Data teacher share of K-12 staff FTE. It assumes every group has the same vacancy rate, which is not true. Treat it as context for the pooled rate, not a count of teacher vacancies.
