The context right now

Schools compete for workers with other employers, so it is hard to know what to make of schools' hiring problems without some sense of how other employers are doing. That is not the only context that matters. Which positions are hard to fill, in which schools, and how staffing levels are changing matter at least as much, and later sections take those up. But whether schools' hiring problems are unique to schools cannot be understood without considering the economy as a whole, and that comparison is often missing from discussions of "teacher shortages."

Education and the whole labor market since 2000

There is no ongoing national data series on unfilled positions in public schools specifically. The closest thing in federal labor-market data is the Job Openings and Labor Turnover Survey's (JOLTS) state & local government education industry. It combines public school districts with public colleges and universities, and it includes all of their employees, not only teachers. Below, it is compared with the economy as a whole and with the rest of state and local government.

How education compares with other industries

An industry is not an occupation. Public education employs bus drivers, custodians, paraprofessionals, and administrators as well as teachers, and those positions are not equally hard to fill. A change in the education industry's openings rate therefore need not reflect a change in teacher vacancies. More on teachers specifically below.

Public education employment

Vacancies are only one part of the picture. Schools can report more unfilled positions because they are trying to employ more people, not only because people are harder to find. In recent years, pandemic relief funds and other revenue allowed many districts to hire more staff even as enrollment fell. The chart indexes employment to February 2020, the last month before the pandemic.

What states actually count

Because there is no ongoing federal collection of unfilled school positions, the most direct evidence comes from states. Only a minority of states publish anything usable, and they do not all measure vacancies the same way. This section uses seven states with vacancy rates (Colorado, Illinois, Iowa, Michigan, Pennsylvania, South Carolina, and Virginia) and one that publishes counts only (Nebraska). Some have only a year or two of data. Each figure describes a school year, using the reporting period given in its definition below, and is published months after the fact. Recent figures were checked against each agency's own publication, and the provenance page records exactly what was verified and what was carried over from earlier work. None is interpolated.

What counts as a "vacancy"

Data sources do not agree on what counts as a vacancy. Some count positions that are unfilled on a particular day, one counts a year's worth of unfilled job postings, and one counts only positions left empty all year. Some count a position as vacant even if a substitute is covering it, while others count it as filled. Differences like these can matter as much as differences in actual staffing conditions, so each number below is best read alongside its definition.

Latest rate and trend by state

Trends within a state are more informative than comparisons across states. Colorado counts only positions left unfilled for the whole year. Illinois, Pennsylvania, and Virginia count positions unfilled in the fall, and Michigan also counts positions held for teachers on leave. Iowa counts a year of full-time teaching postings, including positions covered by substitutes. South Carolina includes counselors, librarians, and other service staff, and Nebraska combines public and private schools. These are related measures, not the same measure, and eight states are not a national estimate. For that reason, the table lists states alphabetically rather than ranking them.

Teachers and other school staff

Pooled figures can hide differences in vacancy rates across staff categories. Illinois publishes filled and unfilled positions separately for different kinds of school staff.

Unfilled is not the same as "not fully qualified"

A position occupied by a teacher on an emergency or provisional license, or by a teacher working outside their certification area, is staffed. Some data sources nevertheless classify occupied positions as unfilled because of the holder's credentials or employment status. Whether a teacher holds a particular credential is a separate question from whether a position is staffed, and certification status is generally a weak indicator of how effective teachers are, though some licensure tests, particularly in math, are modestly predictive (Goldhaber, 2007; Kane, Rockoff, & Staiger, 2008; Goldhaber, 2024, viewed October 10, 2026). Counts that combine the two are broader measures. They can show that districts had trouble finding staff with particular qualifications, but interpreting them as empty positions overstates vacancies, and the counts alone do not establish how effectively the positions are staffed.

What these data cannot say

  • There is no ongoing national count of teacher vacancies. JOLTS measures industries, not occupations. NCES's School Pulse Panel, a survey of public schools, estimated that about 3% of teaching positions were vacant in October 2023 and October 2024 (4% in October 2022), though NCES cautions that questionnaire changes make comparisons across those years uncertain, and its last staffing wave was October 2024. Any other national teacher figure is either a model (like the illustration above, labeled as one) or an aggregation of unlike state definitions.
  • State vacancy rates are not rankings. States define and measure vacancies differently, so changes within a state over time are more informative than comparisons between states.
  • Vacancies depend on demand as well as supply. A district that adds positions can report more vacancies even if hiring has not gotten harder, and when budgets tighten, vacancies can fall even if nothing about the supply of candidates has improved.
  • Annual snapshots miss a lot. Most state data describe a single point in the fall and are published months later. They cannot show how positions were filled over the course of the year, or which positions were hard to fill but filled by October.
  • These are mostly data about quantity, not quality. Vacancy data tell us whether schools could fill the positions they wanted to fill, not how well those positions were filled or how much students learned. Credential categories do not settle the quality question either.
  • Averages hide a great deal. Staffing problems vary between subjects, districts, and schools. In Illinois in fall 2022, for example, about half of districts reported no teacher vacancies at all while a tenth reported that 7.5% or more of their teaching positions were unfilled, and vacancy rates differed about as much between schools in the same district as between districts (Bruno, 2026). Disadvantaged students tend to attend the schools with the highest vacancy rates.
  • Computed rates carry their conventions. Where this page computes a rate from published components, it says so, and the components are in the downloadable data.
  • JOLTS is revised. The latest month is preliminary and each month is revised once. Annual benchmark revisions can change the whole history. This page re-pulls the full history every update.

Methods, sources, and data

Rates. Rates on this page adjust for the size of an employer or workforce, so they can be followed over time and set beside industries of very different sizes. But their numerators, denominators, and reporting periods differ, and expressing something as a percentage does not by itself make two measures comparable. JOLTS job openings rates are openings ÷ (employment + openings) on the last business day of the month; hires, quits, and layoff rates are events during the month ÷ employment. State rates follow each state's own convention, described in each state's definition above: most are unfilled ÷ (filled + unfilled) at a point in the fall, but Illinois pairs this year's unfilled positions with last year's filled positions, Iowa divides a year of unfilled postings by employed teachers, and Pennsylvania's rate is computed here from positions and headcounts.

National data come from the BLS Job Openings and Labor Turnover Survey and Current Employment Statistics, retrieved from the BLS Public Data API (economy-wide JOLTS series JTS000000000000000JOR and related, CPS unemployment LNS13000000 and LNS14000000, CES employment CES0000000001, CES9093161101, CES9092161101, and industry series JTSiiiiii000000000XXX, including 923000 state & local education and 929000 state & local excluding education). Seasonally adjusted except the October state benchmarks, which use the not-seasonally-adjusted series JTU923000000000000JOR and JTU000000000000000JOR. An automated job refreshes these data weekly, so new JOLTS releases appear within days.

School Pulse Panel estimates come from the NCES "Staff Vacancies" workbook (nces.ed.gov/surveys/spp), re-checked automatically for new waves. They are shown as NCES publishes them: percent of positions vacant, rounded to whole percents, with standard errors used for the confidence intervals in tooltips. NCES's flags are carried through: estimates it says to interpret with caution are marked, and its warning that questionnaire changes affect comparisons across waves is noted wherever the waves are compared.

Staff and enrollment come from NCES Digest of Education Statistics Table 213.10, built from the Common Core of Data: full-time-equivalent staff by type and pupils per staff member for public schools, 50 states and DC, fall 2000 on. Enrollment is computed from NCES's own teacher counts and pupils-per-teacher ratio, so staff and students come from the same collection, with staff measured consistently in FTE and students as fall enrollment. This differs from the BLS employment chart, which counts payroll jobs and mixes K-12 with other local education employers. The rates relative to their own history divide each JOLTS series by its own January 2015 – December 2019 average, the same baseline the summary at the top uses.

State data are curated by hand from state agency publications (sources listed under each state above) and updated as states release new figures. The provenance page records, state by state, which figures were checked against the agency's own publication and which were carried over without re-checking. Where a state publishes a rate, the published rate is shown. Where it publishes only the components, the rate is computed and labeled. Where no denominator exists, no rate is shown.

Full documentation of every series (who publishes it, the exact file, how it is retrieved, every transformation, and known problems) is on the data provenance page.

Composition illustration. The breakdown of state & local education openings is an illustration under equal vacancy rates, shown as shares rather than counts.
